Eric Putney

Postdoctoral Associate at the New High Energy Theory Center, Rutgers University

I use machine learning to study astronomical and cosmological evidence for dark matter.

I grew up in Vancouver, WA, across the river from Portland. In 2016 I moved to Albuquerque, NM to get my bachelor's in physics at UNM, and graduated in 2020. I recently defended my Ph.D. thesis at Rutgers University, in New Brunswick, NJ, on building ML models for measuring dark matter in our galaxy.

Generally speaking, I am interested in any and all probes of dark matter physics. I mainly focus on astronomical and cosmological probes, where there is tons of open data and analyses to be done.

In my free time I listen to music, work on my home lab, and play video games. Every day I try to make my life a little more sustainable and independent from large corporate services. It's a long and often non-linear process, but it makes me feel good.

Research

Much of my work on dark matter has focused on its signatures in the Milky Way. Dark matter halos formed in the early universe pulled in visible matter to make stars, galaxies, and everything else. Today our Milky Way sits inside a dark matter halo, giving us an incredible laboratory to study it. We do this in two ways.

Direct detection: look for scattering of dark matter particles off of visible matter. This depends on how much dark matter you expect there to be in a room at any given time. I measured this local density first in 2023, using a relatively crude solution to the six-dimensional Boltzmann equation, and again in 2025, where we correct for dust and provide a 3D map of the local potential.

Indirect detection: look for dark matter turning into some kind of visible Standard Model messenger, which could be light, cosmic rays, or neutrinos. I am interested in the Galactic Center GeV Excess, a long-documented excess of gamma rays from the center of the Milky Way that might be produced by dark matter annihilation.

Now

Broadly speaking, I study dark matter in the galaxy and in the evolution of the universe. Right now I am working on physics-inspired neural network models of cosmic ray transport in the Milky Way, aimed at better γ-ray background modeling for the Galactic Center GeV excess, with Yujin Park, David Shih, and Matthew Buckley at Rutgers and Tracy Slatyer and Christopher Chen at MIT.

I am also working on an interesting form of interacting, viscous dark radiation that arises in a "dark QCD" model of cosmology and could help resolve the Hubble tension, with Nico Fernandez and Matt Buckley.

Finally, I have a long-standing interest in galactic dynamics and Gaia, most recently through ClearPotential, which models the three-dimensional distribution of dark matter around the Sun. We are looking forward to Gaia DR4 and are excited to extend this analysis toward the Galactic Center.
